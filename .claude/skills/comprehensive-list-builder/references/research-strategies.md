# Research Strategies for Exhaustive Lists

The goal of research here is different from normal research. You're not trying to *answer* a
question — you're trying to **union together everything that exists** in a space so the long
tail doesn't slip through. That changes tactics: breadth and triangulation beat depth on any
single source.

## The core move: triangulate across independent taxonomies

Find 2–4 *independent* structured breakdowns of the domain and merge them. Independence
matters — three articles that copied each other give false confidence. The items one source
omits, another usually includes; the union is far more complete than any single list, and the
disagreements between sources are exactly where the long tail lives.

## Where authoritative taxonomies tend to live

Most domains already have someone whose job was to enumerate them. Look for:

- **Government / standards classifications.** These are professionally exhaustive.
  - Household spending → US Bureau of Labor Statistics *Consumer Expenditure Survey* categories.
  - Industries / business types → NAICS or SIC codes.
  - Occupations → BLS Standard Occupational Classification.
  - Products → HS/HTS codes, UNSPSC.
  - Look for the analogous scheme in your domain — there usually is one.
- **Regulatory / compliance frameworks** when the list is about requirements, risks, or controls.
- **Industry-standard frameworks and "bodies of knowledge."**
- **Competitor / incumbent products.** What categories does an existing app, catalog, or
  template already expose? Their category nav is a free taxonomy someone refined with users.
- **"Ultimate guide" / "complete list" / "checklist" content.** Often padded, but good for the
  long tail. Treat as raw material to filter, not gospel.
- **Comparison and aggregator sites**, marketplace category trees, and template galleries
  (e.g. budgeting-template category lists, e-commerce category menus).

## Search patterns that surface the long tail

Vary the phrasing — different queries surface different items:

- `"complete list of" <domain>` / `"comprehensive list of" <domain>`
- `<domain> categories` / `<domain> taxonomy` / `<domain> classification`
- `types of <thing>` and `<thing> examples`
- `<domain> checklist` / `<domain> template`
- `unexpected <domain>` / `commonly forgotten <domain>` / `hidden <domain>` — these
  explicitly target the items people miss (e.g. "commonly forgotten monthly expenses").
- For a branch that fans out, search that branch directly: instead of trusting your memory of
  what a hardware store sells, search `hardware store product categories`.

## Working a branch that fans out

When a category turns out to be internally diverse (the "hardware store" case), don't expand
it from memory — run a dedicated search for *that sub-domain's* own taxonomy. A single
top-level category can have a richer source list than the whole rest of the tree, and that's
fine if it's relevant to the objective.

## Track provenance as you go

Note where each branch or non-obvious item came from. This is cheap in the moment and pays
off twice: it makes the final completeness claim auditable, and during the step-5 gap hunt it
lets you see which sources you've already mined and which fresh ones to diff against.

## Knowing when to stop

You've researched enough when **new sources stop adding new items** — when the next taxonomy
you check is almost entirely already covered. That saturation point, not a fixed number of
searches, is the signal that you've captured the bulk of the space. Note any branches that
never reached saturation as known thin spots.
